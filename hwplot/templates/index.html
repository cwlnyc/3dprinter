1 Teach it your handwriting

One time only. It learns each letter from a sample sheet you fill in by hand.

  1. Download the sample sheet (PDF, {{ pages }} pages)
  2. Print it at 100% / “Actual size”, not “Fit to page”.
  3. Write one character in each box, the character printed under it, sitting on the little tick marks like a line on paper. Tails of g, y, p hang below the ticks.
  4. Use the same pen or pencil you'll put in the printer, writing normally. Don't touch the box edges.
  5. Every character is on there twice. Write both: the output mixes them so it doesn't look stamped.
  6. Photograph each page: flat on a table, good light, no shadow of your phone, and all four black corner squares in the picture. Any angle is fine.

Each upload adds to what it already knows. Fill in another sheet any time, even with a different pen or on a different day, and the letters get more varied. Uploading the same page twice does nothing.

2 Print the pen holder

The part that holds a pen or pencil next to the printer's nozzle. One printed piece; the two thin strips act as a spring so the pen presses lightly and evenly.

Pick the file for what you're writing with. Measure the barrel with a ruler where it'll be clamped (about 3 cm up from the tip).

Print it

  • PLA or PETG, 0.2 mm layers (the strips are exactly 3 layers thick, so this matters), 4 walls, 30% infill, no supports.
  • Print it the way the file is oriented, with the flat plate standing upright.

Put it together

  1. Push an M3 nut into the slot on top of the clamp block.
  2. Screw an M3 × 10 mm screw in from the front; it presses on the pen to hold it.
  3. Bolt the plate to the side or front of your printer's print head (fan shroud or carriage) with two M3 screws. The slots fit any pair of holes 22–44 mm apart. Many printers have spare holes there; otherwise use the fan-shroud screws.
  4. Slide the pen in so its tip sits about 2 mm below the nozzle, then tighten the screw.
  5. Keep the hotend cold and take the filament out. Nothing gets heated.
Pencil? Use the 8 mm file. A mechanical pencil works best: it never needs sharpening, so the line stays the same width all page. With a wooden pencil, sharpen it first and expect lighter lines; lower z_down by 0.3 mm to press harder.

3 Set up your printer

One time only. These tell the program where the pen actually is.

  1. Bed size: from your printer's spec sheet (Ender 3: 220 × 220).
  2. Pen offset: the printer moves the nozzle, but the pen is beside it. Home the printer, move to X100 Y100, lower Z until the pen makes a dot on a scrap sheet. With the nozzle still there, measure from the point straight under the nozzle to the dot. Right of it = positive X, towards the back = positive Y.
  3. Pen-down height (z_down): lower Z in 0.1 mm steps until the pen just draws, then go 0.5 mm further so the spring presses gently. Pen-up (z_up) is about 3 mm above that.
  4. Save, then download the test pattern and run it on scrap paper. It draws a 30 mm square with a cross. Faint or broken lines mean lower z_down; gouged or wobbly lines mean raise it.

4 Scan the homework

Every time. Claude reads the sheet, works out the answers, and finds where each one goes.

  • Photograph the whole page on a darker table so its edges stand out.
  • Good light, no shadows, nothing on top of the sheet. Sharp enough that you could read it yourself.
  • One page per photo. Do double-sided sheets as two runs.
  • Takes 1–3 minutes, since it checks its working.
  • Costs roughly: Sonnet 5–8¢ a sheet, Opus about 20¢, Haiku under 1¢. The real cost shows after each sheet.

The key is kept in memory only while this program runs, and never written to disk. Get one at console.anthropic.com.
